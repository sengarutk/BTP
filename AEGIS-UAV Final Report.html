Perception for small camera drones: where to compute, how fresh, at what energy and heat

A camera drone has to detect objects in its video, live. This project built a three-tier drone testbed, a family of adaptive controllers that decide per frame where to run detection, and measured them over shared Wi-Fi and a real drone mesh network, with the standard streaming-accuracy metric on aerial video.

Hardware: Raspberry Pi 5 camera drones · Jetson Orin Nano edge drone · laptop ground station (RTX 4050) · Wi-Fi and an ad-hoc batman-adv mesh (AegisSwarm). Data: VisDrone2019. Detailed lab record: the AEGIS-UAV Results Brief (25 parts); code: uav-split-perception/.

The work answers four questions with measurements:

  1. Where should the drone compute? Off the drone. A Pi 5 needs 0.3–3 s per frame and throttles within about a minute; the best onboard detector (YOLO11n) reaches 0.047 streaming AP with the forecaster, while offloading over the mesh reaches 0.18–0.21. Sending a JPEG beats splitting the network in every placement measured ; INT8 split computing is lossless, but its payload is 2–12× a JPEG. Learned and jointly trained codes beat JPEG only below a break-even goodput that a tested rule predicts (0 exceptions in every check): below about 2 Mbit/s on the Pi, and 1.5 Mbit/s even on the Jetson once the camera can use low-delay video (4.14).
  2. How should the drone decide? Per frame, using every computer in parallel, with an objective that values a result by the accuracy it still has when it is used. Adaptive control beats fixed offloading once links fail (mesh mission: 0.186 against 0.105) and survives total blackouts on the Pi alone.
  3. How fast do aerial detections go stale, and can that be undone? They lose 58 % of their accuracy within 100 ms. A cheap forecaster that follows the camera's and each object's motion keeps 60 % at 267 ms (7 % without) and roughly doubles streaming accuracy. The scheduler must use the decay curve after forecasting (+0.0045 sAP, 20 pairs).
  4. What do energy and heat cost? Onboard inference is about 80× the energy of sending a JPEG and drives the fanless Pi into throttling. Pricing the drone's measured power (dual ascent) and the freshness of its detections keeps the same or better accuracy with about 1–1.5 W less, no throttling and 98 % freshness. A heat price adds nothing beyond that; pricing the edge drone's energy saves only 0–10 % of its power (about 4 % at a budget that costs almost no accuracy).

01The problem

A small drone carries a camera and a weak computer. It must find people and vehicles in its video while it flies, and its operators need the newest possible picture. For every frame (30 per second) it can run a detector itself, send the frame to a stronger computer on another drone (edge drone), or send it to the ground station, and it can choose the model size, image resolution and JPEG quality. Links fade, congest and break; the drone's battery and temperature are limited.

What "good" means. A detection is useful only while it still matches the scene. The project scores what the drone holds at every frame: the newest detection it has, compared with the ground truth of the current frame. This is streaming AP (sAP), the standard metric of streaming perception (Li et al., ECCV 2020), computed as mAP@[.5:.95] on VisDrone video. Freshness is reported as P(AoI ≤ 300 ms), the share of time the drone holds a detection younger than 300 ms.

03How results were measured

  • Test stream: VisDrone2019-VID validation, 7 clips, 2,846 frames, about 108,000 labelled objects, played at 30 fps at 640 px. Controller inputs (accuracy per setting, decay curves, forecaster tuning) come only from VisDrone-DET val and VID train; the test clips are never used for tuning.
  • Metrics: standard sAP (newest-result holding rule) with and without the forecaster; mAP of delivered results; P(AoI ≤ 300 ms), 95th-percentile age; deadline-met throughput; measured Pi and Jetson power, temperature, throttled-time ratio from the firmware flag.
  • Design: strategies run in shuffled order, the same scenarios and repetitions for each, compared only within one campaign (same code, same day). Scenarios include a clean link, scripted missions (congestion, ground blackout, edge link loss, total blackout, recovery), forced multihop, out of range, and 24-minute missions.
  • Statistics: paired differences over scenario × repetition, 95 % Student-t intervals (corrected on 26 October: earlier summaries used 1.96 for 11 or more pairs, see section 8), exact sign-flip permutation p-values, Holm correction within each campaign.
  • Two scorings, labelled. The Wi-Fi campaigns of 6–13 October (4.3–4.5, the Wi-Fi row of 4.6 and the controller table) are quoted as originally scored: COCO-trained detectors and a quality-aware holding rule. Every one was re-scored with the standard sAP and VisDrone-trained detectors (4.11): the switch of detectors reversed no verdict, the Holm correction withdrew two earlier claims (v2's lead in the 108-run campaign, v5 over fixed JPEG offloading), and the energy-price gains grew (+0.0074 → +0.019 and +0.0056 → +0.017 sAP). The mesh campaigns, Part 17 onward and the Pareto analysis (4.10, which covers the Wi-Fi campaigns too) use the standard sAP with VisDrone detectors throughout.
  • Checks of the measuring itself: the sAP scorer reproduces the official ultralytics validator (0.0967 against 0.0976); the simulator predicts 528 measured phases with correlation 0.947; every earlier campaign was re-scored with the standard metric and again with VisDrone-trained detectors; every run was audited for a receiver fault found in October (below).

04Results

4.1 Where to compute: send the image, don't split the network

Across 19 detectors the smallest split payload (INT8 + zstd at layer 4, 310.7 KiB for YOLOv8s) is 2.0–11.5× a JPEG frame, and the drone must also run the first layers (210–252 ms on the Pi). No split beats a JPEG in the model of all 1,150 placements over the Pi, the Jetson and the laptop, nor in live runs of the important ones: the best split (layer 1) delivered 7.0 frames/s (2.5 on time) against 15.2 (14.7 on time) for a JPEG to the laptop. INT8 splitting loses at most 0.04 mAP points on COCO and at most 0.0004 mAP on VisDrone; image codecs lose 2.4–5.4 times more accuracy on aerial images than on COCO (JPEG q50 −24 % against −8 %, q90 −4.0 % against −0.7 %), because small objects lose their detail first.

A learned bottleneck changes the bits, not the verdict (30 Oct). A small encoder and decoder trained at layer 2 or 4 of the VisDrone YOLOv8s, with the detector frozen (distillation of its own neck outputs, no labels), needs 23–76 % fewer bytes than JPEG for the same accuracy, measured on the same VisDrone frames, which no post-hoc split does. It tops out at 0.253 mAP (105 kB) against 0.256 for a JPEG q90 of 78 kB. In the system model it beats the image only with fast onboard compute and a slow link (YOLOv8s at 40 ms per frame: up to 17.3 Mbit/s), and on the measured Pi (758 ms) only up to 0.6 Mbit/s, where no option delivers more than 2.7 accuracy-weighted on-time results per second; above that, running the first layers and the encoder onboard costs more time than the smaller payload saves. (Results Brief 19.12.)

4.3 Adaptive, multi-server scheduling

Using the Pi, the Jetson and the laptop in parallel and choosing the setting per frame (v2) gave 55–62 % more accuracy-weighted deadline-met throughput than the best fixed strategy on impaired links, best in 8 of 8 pairs, and kept about 10 detections/s through a total blackout on the Pi alone. On real streaming accuracy (108 Wi-Fi runs, original scoring) it was ahead of every other policy on average (+4.5 % to +201 %), significantly against onboard, the Jetson and rule-based fallback; with the standard metric, the forecaster and the Holm correction (4.11) it stays significantly ahead only of onboard and the fixed Jetson path, and the fixed laptop has the highest mean (not significant). On a clean link a fixed strong server is as good; adaptation pays when links fail.

4.4 Staleness on aerial video, and the forecaster

A detection kept unchanged loses 58 % of its accuracy within 100 ms and 93 % at 267 ms, because the camera itself moves. Forecaster v1 (object velocity) keeps 47 % at 267 ms and raised sAP for all 9 policies (median +32 % to +67 %); v2, which first aligns the camera motion, keeps 60 %, beats v1 in all 144 runs and roughly doubles sAP (on the mesh, normal link: 0.117 → 0.206). Re-measured with a detector that never saw the training clips (trained on VisDrone-DET only), the decay curve is the same and the forecaster in use ranks 2nd of 25 settings, 0.1 % from the best.

4.5 The objective must match the forecaster

A scheduler built on the steep no-forecast curve over-values fast, low-accuracy results and lost (v3: −14 % against v2). Rebuilt on the forecaster's flatter curve it beat v2 by +0.0045 ± 0.0038 sAP over 20 pairs (p = 0.02 in the original scoring; with the standard holding rule +0.0038 ± 0.0039, Holm p = 0.22, not significant; VisDrone re-scoring +0.015, Holm p = 0.007) with 99.5–99.8 % freshness. Ablations: removing the decay entirely costs −0.0054 sAP, using the wrong curve −0.0059.

4.6 Energy, freshness and heat priced together

Onboard YOLOv8s costs 2.2 J per frame against about 0.03 J to send the frame as a JPEG. The Pi's steady temperature rises 11.3 °C per watt; it starts throttling at about 3.5–3.7 W. Earlier controllers ran it at 4.2–5.3 W, with onboard inference taking 40–54 % of the power for 3–5 % of the accuracy. Counting the edge drone the adaptive controllers keep in the air, fixed offloading to the ground stays 2.5–2.7× more energy-efficient for the whole system; the energy price saves the camera drone's power and heat, not the swarm's.

4.7 Relay and hierarchical designs on the mesh

With the camera out of the ground station's range, a direct path delivers nothing while the relay Pi → Jetson → laptop delivers 0.161 sAP and v4.1 switches to it by itself (0.143). On the mesh with a direct camera-to-ground link, v4.1 beats the hierarchical chain in 6 of 6 pairs (0.193 against 0.146, p = 0.031); when every packet must cross the Jetson (multihop), the chain is ahead (0.151 against 0.137, 3 of 3). The better design depends on the topology. The two topologies were measured in campaigns a day apart and the multihop result has 3 pairs (p = 0.25, the smallest possible), so the reversal is measured but not yet isolated in one campaign.

4.9 Many drones

In a simulator rebuilt from 44 real runs (correlation 0.947 over 528 measured phases), fixed offloading collapses as a swarm grows; adaptive control degrades gracefully, and a ground-side admission coordinator turns the collapse of 20 Pi drones into 4.15 detections/s each and, at 10 drones, lifts the worst-served drone from 0 to 7.65 frames/s. Adding each radio's own link health removes its one real loss. On hardware with two cameras, sharing the ground station costs the Pi 11–19 % of its accuracy, and adaptive control beats fixed offloading in 6 of 6 pairs. Re-weighted with VisDrone accuracy, the simulation keeps its best strategy in 93 of 96 cells (of the other 3, 2 are exact ties and 1 is within the confidence interval).

4.10 The trade-off: accuracy against power

Over 633 runs with logged Pi power (VisDrone re-scoring, standard sAP with the forecaster), a priced controller is on the accuracy-versus-power Pareto front in every campaign (in the main campaign it is v5's linear-decay variant; v5 itself trails it by 0.0003 sAP) and is the most accurate strategy in every campaign. Literature schedulers that offload or retry aggressively (shortest queue, confidence trigger, offloading reward) are both 0.026–0.050 sAP less accurate and 1.5–1.7 W more power-hungry. The cheap end of the front belongs to fixed offloading and a freshness threshold, which in the short runs save about 0.6 W by giving up 17–28 % of accuracy, and fail when the link fails.

4.11 Robustness of the evidence

  • Detector weights. All 776 earlier runs re-scored with VisDrone-trained detections: sAP about 2.4× higher, rankings nearly unchanged (rank correlation 0.92–1.00), 0 of 49 paired verdicts reversed, 3 became significant in the direction already seen.
  • Receiver fault. A thread race in the camera's link code could silently drop returning results. Audited across the 824 runs made before the fix: it hit 16 (2 %), and removing them changes no verdict.
  • Laptop network. The laptop's Ethernet dropped sparse packets (energy-efficient Ethernet); worked around with a keep-awake ping, and every mesh run was checked against a 1-second ping log (5 runs discarded and re-run).

4.12 Against a published planner, and the distance from the best schedule (trace replay)

An AccuMO-style planner (MobiCom 2023: one setting per server for the next 30 frames, 252 plans, the content-conditioned decay) was re-implemented in the trace replay. Against v4.1 it tied on the mission (+0.0018) and was slightly ahead on random link faults (+0.0044; +0.0045 on fresh seeds) and over 24-minute missions (+0.0014), but a LEAF-style fixed weight was ahead by as much, and the replay is known to favour such low-onboard policies by about 0.005 sAP. So the claim narrows to: a greedy priced choice does as well as a 30-frame planner. The planner now runs in the board code (adaptive2.py --policy accumo); its mesh campaign is scripted. In the same replay v4.1 reaches 97–100 % of a hindsight per-phase oracle (88 % when the faults change every 4 s), gains nothing from a clairvoyant link, and its own settings are not hand-tuned: only the energy target moves accuracy, and 2.8 W holds on fresh seeds. (Results Brief 19.8–19.11.)

4.13 Many cameras on one edge drone, and the camera Pi's clock (10 Nov, from logged data)

Overload. A queueing model fed with measured stage times (overload_model.py; Results Brief 19.13, rewritten 13 Nov) asks what happens when cameras offer the Jetson more frames than it can take. The Jetson's path carries about 17 frames/s over the Wi-Fi access point (the radio is the limit), 26 over one mesh hop (its GPU) and 12 relayed. A naive queue turns overload into nothing: one Pi at 30 frames/s gets 0 results on time, with a median latency of 28 s. v4.1 sends its newest frame once the previous one has left the radio, so one Pi never overloads the path: 12.2 on-time results/s on Wi-Fi, 22.1 on the mesh. Its modelled 74 ms median matches the boards' 74 ms. It serves up to 3 Pis per Jetson on Wi-Fi and 5 on a mesh hop, then collapses. A coordinator on the Jetson that pulls one frame ahead keeps it at full capacity with any number of Pis. With several Jetsons, capacity grows only if each has its own channel. The model is far too optimistic for relays: the boards already fail with one Pi (12.5 % on time), and computing at the relay is the fix (the hierarchical mode, 100 % on time). (The 10 Nov version had called stop-and-wait v4.1's rule; corrected.)

Clock speed. Every mesh run logged the Pi's clock (clock_effect_analysis.py; 19.14). With v4.1 the Pi is at its full 2.4 GHz 60 % of the time and slowed by the governor because it is idle 40 %, never throttled; without prices it is throttled 86 % of the time. Pinning the full clock would save at most about 6 ms on laptop-served frames and at most +0.0009 sAP, and part of that is confounding. Throttling is the harmful kind: it makes onboard frames 12–27 % slower. Verdict: keep the default governor; prevent throttling, not idle down-clocking.

4.14 When can a split pay off? A tested rule (paper 1, 10–12 Nov)

The learned bottleneck of 4.1 beats JPEG on bits but rarely end to end. Paper 1 now says exactly where. Proposition (break-even goodput): a code k can beat every image setting only below Bbe(k), the payload rate of the cheapest image setting that matches its accuracy, at the code's own frame time, or that setting's deadline rate. Two corollaries follow. Bbe does not depend on the code's own size, so no better coder (even one at the rate–distortion limit) raises it; it only adds wins below it. A split that switches codes slot by slot is bounded by the largest of their Bbe. The rule was then put through every test a reviewer is likely to ask for. Predictions were committed before each run, and the results were written against them (docs/*predictions*.md).

What it means. The paper's verdict holds and is now sharper. A split pays off only below a goodput the rule predicts from the code's accuracy, the image codec's rate curve and the device's speed. That window is below about 2 Mbit/s on a Pi (0.7–1.9 Mbit/s, depending on the code and the image baseline), and once the camera can use low-delay video it shrinks to 1.5 Mbit/s even on the Jetson. On a board like the Pi a split never saves energy. Details: paper/drafts/design_rule.tex (8 pages in a test build, every number generated).

4.15 Guarantees for the priced controller, and where heat still comes from (paper 2, 10–12 Nov)

  • Theorem 1 (power, every run). Mean power stays below the budget plus a price term (at most 0.21 W over a 4-minute run, 0.035 W over 24 minutes) plus the updates where the price was held or capped. It follows from the update rule, so what had to be checked was the rule. All 66,655 logged updates of the 376 priced runs (10 campaigns) follow it (0 unexplained), so the bound holds on every run. The freshness, heat and Jetson prices follow their stated rules on all 132,042 of their logged updates too; their caps were added on 13 Oct, and 36 earlier runs ran without them. It is a decomposition, not a cap: 364 of the 376 runs averaged above budget, by at most 0.07 W on the mesh and 0.15 W over 24 minutes.
  • Theorem 2 (accuracy gap, idealised). The standard drift-plus-penalty trade: a gap of order η against a price term of order 1/η. The hardware breaks its assumptions in six stated ways, so it is a guide, not a measurement.
  • Budget and temperature. The measured thermal model, 34.4 + 12.7·P °C (τ = 149 s), predicts the mean temperature of all 16 runs of 20 minutes or more within 1.6 °C. A budget of 2.8 W means about 70 °C; staying below the 80 °C throttle point needs B ≤ 3.6 W.
  • Where heat still comes from. On 24-minute Wi-Fi missions with total blackouts, the board was still at or above 80 °C 19–27 % of the time. The validated trace replay traces this to the controller's blackout hold (onboard work while every link is down), at every budget from 2.4 to 4.5 W. Capping the hold at 78 °C gives 0 % hot time at the same sAP (replay; board test pending). The paper's heat claim is scoped to this.
  • A warmer room. At +10 °C the 2.8 W budget is too high, and raising the price cap alone leaves 42 % hot time. A budget that estimates the room's offset online, together with a higher cap, gives 0 % hot time at +5 and +10 °C, at 94 % and 89 % freshness.
  • Theorem 3 (hard guard, peak temperature). If no onboard inference starts at or above a guard temperature, the peak is bounded: 79.9 °C with the lab constants, and it stays below the 85 °C throttling limit for a room up to 14.5 °C warmer. Its converse: no controller can keep the board cooler than its offload-only floor. In the replay the bound held on all 20 guard runs, with no hot time up to +10 °C. At +15 °C the floor alone averages 79.9 °C and the board is hot 84 % of the time, as the converse predicts.
  • Settings swept (285 replay runs, predictions committed first): the step size trades the price term against the gap as the theorems say, with no measurable sAP effect. The replay reproduces mean power (r = 0.99) and the ranking of hot time (r = 0.98), but not its level (off by 16 points), so its hot-time numbers are comparisons, not predictions.
  • A learned baseline. A constrained deep-RL policy (PPO-Lagrangian, budget 2.8 W) keeps the budget (2.43 W) but trails v4.1 on all three scenarios: −0.0100 ± 0.0014 sAP over 24 minutes, −0.0124 ± 0.0039 on 2-minute missions, −0.0038 ± 0.0034 with random faults. Two earlier training attempts failed and are reported.
  • Noisy relays (calibrated swarm simulator; 144 settings with 3–8 dB shadowing and 0–10 % loss per hop): the adaptive policy relays at most 1.9 % of frames, and relaying changes its rate by at most 4 %. The shared access point, not the relay link, limits the swarm.
  • Calibration. Over 44,524 offloaded frames, v4.1 predicted a 94.4 % chance of meeting the deadline and 98.5 % did: cautious, not optimistic.

Paper 2 builds at 9 pages. The hard guard, the capped hold and the room-following budget are replay results until the board session confirms them.

05Negative results

  • The original staleness-aware v3 did not beat v2 (fixed by matching the objective to the forecaster).
  • On a clean link, a fixed strong server is as good as any adaptive policy.
  • A predictive heat price adds nothing beyond the energy price; stacking every price at once (v5.1) is behind over long missions.
  • Pricing the edge drone's energy saves little of its power, because the Jetson idles at 4.1 W: 0–10 % at a tight 0.5 W budget, which costs 0.016 sAP, and about 4 % at 1.2 W, which costs 0.002.
  • A fixed energy weight ties the adaptive price, but only when set to the value the price finds by itself.
  • Feedback learning in the swarm coordinator did not pay off; the coordinator rarely acts with only two cameras.
  • The design proposed in the original hardware-testing document (split at layer 4 to an edge "mothership") was never on time: 2.7 frames/s against 15.2 for a JPEG sent to the laptop (only the Pi alone and the two-split chain were slower).
  • The constant-goodput break-even bound of paper 1 fails under fading on fast devices (14–24 % of simulated wins above it); only the spread-discounted version holds, and it is empirical, not proved.
  • A coarse JPEG grid overstated where splits pay off (657 against 548 winning cells), and one apparent win of a jointly trained code on the Pi was an artefact of it.
  • Region-of-interest JPEG with server feedback saved nothing at equal accuracy; learned image codecs are too expensive to run on either board.
  • The YOLO11s codes never win on the Pi: their onboard layers leave 12 ms of the deadline.
  • The priced controller still left the board hot 19–27 % of the time on 24-minute missions with blackouts, through its blackout hold; at +10 °C the 2.8 W budget is too high and a higher price cap alone does not fix it.
  • The constrained deep-RL baseline trails v4.1, and its first two training attempts failed (an under-charged radio; a multiplier that wound up until the policy only skipped).

Verdict. Novel as a whole, narrowly stated: no published work found combines drone offloading over a mesh with pricing of the camera drone's measured energy and heat (with freshness), measured on hardware and scored with streaming accuracy. Freshness × accuracy alone (AoPI, TMC 2024) and streaming perception on UAV video alone (StreamFlow, TCSVT 2025) are published, as an external review with Consensus confirmed on 26 October. Ready in substance for a UAV or edge-computing workshop or an IEEE networking conference. A top venue would additionally need two or more camera drones on the mesh, the published scheduler measured on the testbed, and ideally a flight or outdoor test. Since 30 October a learned feature-compression baseline is measured (it beats JPEG on bits, and on the Pi end to end only at the lowest goodputs) and an AccuMO-style planner is re-implemented (in the trace replay, where it ties v4.1, and in the board code, where its campaign is scripted). Fitting now: IEEE IoT Journal, ACM TECS, IEEE ICC/Globecom/WoWMoM, UAV and edge workshops. The closest single work is A²-UAV (INFOCOM 2023; Computer Networks 2025). Since 10 November both papers have been hardened on the laptop. Paper 1 has a tested break-even rule; paper 2 has a power guarantee checked on every logged update, a thermal budget rule, a hard-guard peak bound and a deep-RL baseline (4.14, 4.15). Honest venue view: paper 1 is realistic for IEEE TMC and a strong fit for IoT-J. Paper 2 is more realistic for TMC; INFOCOM only if the board session confirms the capped hold. Full analysis and sources: the AEGIS-UAV Novelty Brief.

07Limitations

  • No flight: video is replayed and the boards sit in one room, one radio hop apart.
  • One camera drone on the mesh; results for larger swarms come from the calibrated simulator.
  • 3 repetitions per mesh cell (in the 24-minute mesh runs 5 for v4.1 and heat, 3 for the Jetson-priced variants; 5 in the Wi-Fi energy campaigns); some controller variants differ only slightly.
  • The laptop joins the mesh through SSH port forwards on the ground radio, which acknowledges data before it reaches the laptop.
  • batman-adv is used as is; real flying-network studies found it can trail Babel and OLSR in throughput and loss, and its penalty here is not characterised.
  • Pi power is the sum of its PMIC rails (a lower bound); the Jetson's power was modelled in the first energy campaign, logged live for every strategy on 11 October and, on the mesh, for the strategies that price it (model within 0.11 W of measurement).
  • The forecaster assumes constant velocity and a similarity transform for the camera; an IMU-assisted or learned forecaster may add more.
  • Learned feature compression is now tested (4.14), but the MPEG feature-coding standard was not run, a learned per-region quality model (AccMPEG) was not tried, and the YOLO11s device times are scaled from YOLOv8s measurements.
  • The video options assume an error-free stream, and their encode times on the boards are scaled from the laptop, not measured.
  • The capped hold, the hard guard and the room-following budget are trace-replay results; the replay ranks hot time correctly but misses its level by 16 points.
